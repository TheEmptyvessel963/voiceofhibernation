## 🎭 Three Kinds of People

Watch how people relate to a skill and you will notice that each one is steered mainly by one of three things. This is a simple mental model of my own, not an official scientific classification, but research supports many parts of it.

**1. The Performer: steered by the audience.** This person shows their skills and knowledge mainly for others. Whether the skill is perfect or imperfect, the goal is to keep the audience impressed. Psychologists call this a *performance goal* (looking capable), as opposed to a *learning goal* (becoming capable) [1]. When the aim is to look good, mistakes feel dangerous, so people stay with what they can already do safely.

**2. The Believer: steered by their own belief.** This person sincerely thinks they are good at something, and often earns a good reputation for it. When the results are only average, the belief covers them comfortably, so nothing forces a correction.

**3. The Seeker: steered by reality.** This person does it neither for the audience nor for a comforting belief. They travel toward the real goal, even when the road is long and hard. Because their base is real, they can shine even after switching to a new area. They carry with them habits that work anywhere.

![Illustration of three cards: the Performer steered by the audience, the Believer steered by their own belief, and the Seeker steered by reality](/assets/images/expertise/three-kinds.svg)
*Three ways to relate to a skill.*

**Two honest notes before we go on.**

- **People move between the three.** The same person can be a Seeker at work, a Performer on social media, and a Believer in an area they have never tested. Success can even push a Seeker toward Belief, because winning makes you trust your feelings more.
- **The model leaves two groups out.** Honest beginners, which everyone once was, and capable people who underrate themselves. In the famous Dunning-Kruger studies, the top scorers rated themselves slightly *lower* than they really performed [2].

So use this model as a mirror for yourself, not as a label for other people. Motives are invisible from the outside.

The rest of this post looks at the evidence behind each type and at what it takes to become a Seeker.

## 🎯 The Believer's Trap: Feeling Skilled vs. Being Skilled

In 1999, psychologists Justin Kruger and David Dunning ran four studies testing people on humor, grammar, and logic, then asked them to guess how well they had done. Those in the bottom quarter scored around the 12th percentile, yet they estimated themselves at about the 62nd [2].

Their explanation was a "dual burden": the skills needed to do something well are often the same skills needed to notice you are doing it badly.

A second finding shows the same trap. In 2002, Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil asked people to rate how well they understood everyday things like zippers and toilets. Then they asked them to explain, step by step, how those things work. After trying, people rated their own understanding lower [3]. **A simple test for the Believer in all of us: try to explain it step by step, with no gaps.**

**A fair warning.** The Dunning-Kruger result is debated. Some researchers argue the pattern is partly a statistical artifact [4], while others replied that those critiques have measurement problems of their own [5]. The popular "Mount Stupid" curve you see online is not from the original paper. The safe takeaway is modest: people are often poor judges of their own skill, especially when they are new to something.

**Why average results don't correct the belief.** In his long study of expert forecasters, Philip Tetlock found that when their predictions failed, many experts explained the failure away instead of changing their theories [6]. In fields where feedback is slow or vague, an average result is easy to cover with confidence.

## 🔑 Key Distinctions

- **Knowing about** vs. **knowing how**
- **Performance in familiar conditions** vs. **performance under novelty**
- **Articulating knowledge** vs. **applying it**

Someone can explain swimming perfectly and still sink. Someone else can swim beautifully and be unable to explain how. Fluent talk is not proof of skill, and skill is not always fluent talk.

## ♟️ What Chess Taught Us: The Seeker's Library

Chess is the classic laboratory for studying expertise, because skill can be measured exactly through ratings and results.

**Step 1: de Groot.** Dutch psychologist Adriaan de Groot asked chess players of different strengths to think aloud while choosing a move. The surprise was that masters did not search dramatically more moves than weaker players. They just *saw* the strong candidate moves almost at once [7].

**Step 2: Chase and Simon.** In 1973, William Chase and Herbert Simon followed up. Players looked at a board for about five seconds, then rebuilt it from memory. Better players did much better with real game positions, but the advantage mostly vanished with randomly placed pieces [8]. (The study used just three players, a master, an advanced player, and a beginner, so later research was needed to confirm the idea.)

So masters don't have a magic memory. They have **chunks**: stored patterns that mean something, like a familiar castled king with its pawn shield. Simon and Gilmartin later estimated that a master holds tens of thousands of such patterns [9].

![Illustration: a beginner sees twelve separate pieces while a master sees the same pieces as three meaningful chunks](/assets/images/expertise/chunking.svg)
*A beginner sees pieces. A master sees patterns.*

![Schematic showing masters recall far more pieces from real positions, but the advantage mostly disappears for random positions](/assets/images/expertise/real-vs-random.svg)
*Schematic of the finding in [8]. Bar heights are illustrative, not the study's real numbers.*

This is what "pattern recognition" really means. It isn't mysterious intuition. It is a large library built from years of seeing the same kinds of situations and learning what happens next. It is also **domain-specific**: the library is built for one field, which is why a switch to a new area means rebuilding it.

## ✅ What Real Expertise Looks Like

Experts tend to:

1. **Know the limits of their knowledge (metacognition).** They say "it depends" and "I'm not sure" more often, and can tell you *when* their method stops working.
2. **Reason differently in their domain.** Beginners analyze step by step. Experts recognize the situation first, then check it. Pattern recognition does the heavy lifting, and deliberate analysis double-checks.
3. **Perform well under time pressure.** Their knowledge is already organized, so they don't have to build the answer from scratch.

## 🚦 Can You Trust an Expert's Gut?

Not always, and this is where the Believer and the Seeker separate. Psychologist Daniel Kahneman, who studied how intuition goes wrong, worked with Gary Klein, who studied how it goes right (for example, in firefighters). After years of debate they agreed on two conditions for trustworthy intuition: the environment must be regular enough to have learnable patterns, and the person must have had real chances to practice with clear feedback. They also stressed that how *sure* someone feels is not a reliable sign of how *right* they are [10].

![A two by two grid showing intuition is trustworthy only when the world is regular and feedback is fast and clear](/assets/images/expertise/trust-intuition.svg)
*Simplified from [10].*

Tetlock tested the other side. He collected about 28,000 predictions from 284 experts on world affairs over roughly two decades. Overall, the experts did little better than simple forecasting rules, and confidence was not a sign of accuracy. The better forecasters were the "foxes," who know many small things and change their minds, rather than the "hedgehogs," who know one big idea and defend it [6]. 🦊🦔

So the honest question is not just "is this person smart?" but "did their field ever give them a fair chance to learn?"

## 📚 Practice Matters, But Not Just Hours

Anders Ericsson and colleagues proposed **deliberate practice**: focused effort on your weak spots, ideally with a teacher and quick feedback [11]. That is why someone can work in a field for 20 years and improve little after the first two.

The popular "10,000 hours" idea oversimplifies it, though. A large meta-analysis of 88 studies found that practice explained very different amounts of skill differences depending on the field [12]:

| Field | Share of performance differences explained by deliberate practice |
|---|---|
| Games (like chess) | 26% |
| Music | 21% |
| Sports | 18% |
| Education | 4% |
| Professions | less than 1% |

The authors concluded practice is important but not as important as some claimed. Ericsson replied that they counted many kinds of practice that don't fit his strict definition [13]. A follow-up study of sports found that practice explained about 18% of differences overall, but only about 1% among elite performers [14]. The fair summary: practice clearly helps, and how much it explains depends on the field and on what counts as "deliberate."

## 🧬 Can Anyone Become an Expert in Any Area?

A common argument goes like this: humans carry all knowledge in their genes, so anyone can shine anywhere if they dig deeply and keep updating. Part of this is right, and part needs adjusting.

**Genes carry instincts, not finished knowledge.** Nobody is born knowing chess or chemistry. Instincts are small, built-in behaviors that survived because they helped ancestors live. A cat covering its waste is one, though kittens probably refine it by watching their mother (as far as I know). Even the popular dog example is less clear than it looks. A survey of 1,571 owners of plant-eating dogs found that only about 9% said their dog often seemed ill beforehand, and only about 22% said it often vomited afterward. The researchers concluded that plant eating is a normal behavior of healthy dogs, not a medicine the dog invents when sick [15].

**Evolution is too slow for skills this new.** Chess is roughly 1,500 years old, writing about 5,000, and computers only decades. No gene can store skills that recent. Babies are born ready to learn language, but they learn whichever language they hear. That is why children need years of learning.

![Three cards showing that skills come from genes (instincts and the ability to learn), your own practice, and teachers and culture](/assets/images/expertise/where-skills-come-from.svg)
*Instinct is slow and narrow. Learning is fast and flexible, but every person has to do it.*

**The better version of the argument:** animals get a few narrow skills built in, while humans traded most of that for flexible learning. That flexibility is why one person can learn farming, chess, or coding. So "almost anyone can become genuinely good at almost any area" is well supported, with limits:

- **Not every area at once.** Time is limited, and chunks are built one field at a time.
- **Not always to the very top.** Practice explains only part of the differences among top performers [12][14]. Even Ericsson's original paper made exceptions for things like height and body size in some sports [11]. Opportunity, teaching, and starting age matter as well.
- **Updates need feedback.** Reading updates without testing yourself can make you feel knowledgeable without getting better.

## 🧭 Is Your Interest Found or Grown?

Some say instinct and intuition tell us which skills we match. The two words mean different things. **Instinct is inherited. Intuition, in expertise research, is learned:** a chess master's sense of the right move comes from years of stored patterns.

An inner pull toward an area is a real compass. Curiosity and enjoyment keep you practicing, and practice is what builds skill. But the feeling of "this is exactly my match" is a weak test:

- Feeling sure is not proof of being right [10], and weak performers often overrate themselves [2].
- Interests are often **grown**, not found. In five studies, O'Keefe, Dweck, and Walton compared people who believe passions are pre-formed and simply discovered with people who believe they are developed. The "found" group was less open to areas outside their existing interests, and their interest dropped more when a new area became hard. The researchers suggest we should develop passions, not wait to find them [16].

So let your interest choose the direction, and let real-world results tell you whether it is working.

## 👨‍👩‍👧 The Push From Others

Interests are also shaped by the people and messages around us. Marketing and social norms influence which careers look prestigious, and parents, often out of love and worry about security, may pass their own dreams on to their children.

India's medical education is a well-documented example. Studies repeatedly report that many students choose medicine under parental pressure, and that a good number later regret it. In one 2024 study, 26.8% of respondents regretted taking up medicine, especially those who joined because of their parents' wishes [17]. The picture is mixed, though: in another study of 150 first-year students, 72.2% chose MBBS out of self-interest, and 14.6% felt regret [18].

Here the three kinds of people connect back. A career chosen mainly for approval and prestige pushes a person toward **Performer** mode from day one. (That is my own reading of the evidence, not a finding of these studies.) Where interest comes first, the road toward **Seeker** mode is easier.

## 📜 A License Is a Starting Line

Someone who practices a skill for five years can learn a great deal of their field. A license then proves they passed a minimum standard. It does not prove expert-level mastery, and the research on experience shows why:

- A big review of 62 evaluations found that 32 of them (52%) reported performance getting worse as doctors' years in practice increased [19].
- A later review of 63 evaluations found the picture mixed: no clear link between experience and overall quality, though safety outcomes, especially in surgery, improved with experience [20].

That matches Kahneman and Klein: years of work only build expertise when you get clear feedback on your results.

**What about the law?** Doctors are not protected simply because they hold a license. Indian law provides civil, criminal, and disciplinary remedies. For a *criminal* case, though, the Supreme Court's *Jacob Mathew* judgment (2005) set a high bar: the negligence must be gross, and courts should first get an independent, competent medical opinion. The reasoning is that medicine involves uncertainty, and honest errors of judgment or bad outcomes are not automatically crimes [21]. Whether the system holds doctors accountable enough is debated. (I am not a lawyer; for a specific case, ask one.)

The fix is not to distrust every professional. It is ongoing checks: peer review, re-testing, tracking outcomes, and second opinions for serious decisions.

## 🥋 The Master Matters

The person you learn from shapes your skills, knowledge, and habits. Benjamin Bloom studied 120 top performers in six fields and found a common pattern in their homes and in their instruction, whatever the field [22]. Ericsson's own definition of deliberate practice includes tasks chosen by a supervising teacher, with immediate feedback [13].

**Vikram and Suriya with Bala.** Vikram struggled for almost a decade without a hit until Bala's debut film *Sethu* (1999) [24]. *Nandha* (2001) was Suriya's big break, and he has said Bala believed in him more than he believed in himself [23]. In *Pithamagan* (2003), Vikram won the National Award for Best Actor [23]. But a director gives more than teaching: he gives the role, the chance, and the timing. Vikram also had big commercial hits with other directors in 2003, and Suriya's *Kaakha Kaakha* came out that year too [25]. And Suriya says Bala's way of working takes more time than he can give now [25], a hint that he took the lessons without copying the whole method.

**Alexander and Aristotle.** Aristotle was invited around 343 BCE to tutor the roughly 13-year-old Alexander [26][27]. He taught subjects like geography, zoology, politics, and medicine [27], and Alexander's habit of reading and his curiosity are often credited to him [26]. But historians generally credit his father Philip II's earlier build-up of Macedonian power, and Alexander's own leadership, for the conquests. Aristotle shaped his mind, not his battlefield skills.

**Adopt the core, not the surface.** Learners usually start by imitating, and that is normal. The real test of whether you got the core of a teaching is whether you can use it in a *new* situation. This matches Chase and Simon: the masters' advantage came from meaningful patterns, not from memorizing surface details.

**How to choose a master:**
1. Look at their students' results, not only their fame.
2. Check that they give clear, honest feedback.
3. Learn their principles, then test them on your own problems.
4. Eventually leave and develop your own way.

A master shortens the path, but the practice is still yours to do.

## 🔍 How to Spot Real Expertise, and Which Kind Are You?

Since we can't always test someone directly, a few questions help:

- ❓ **Can they tell you where their advice fails?** Real experts know the edge cases.
- 🔄 **What would change their mind?** If nothing could, be careful.
- 📈 **Do they have a track record with feedback?** Predictions that were checked matter more than opinions never tested.
- 🌱 **Do they stay useful when the problem is new?** Someone who only handles textbook cases may be trained but not expert.
- 🙋 **Do they stay humble outside their field?** Expertise in one area doesn't carry over to others.

And four questions to ask yourself:

1. When results contradict me, do I change my approach, or explain it away?
2. Could I explain my skill step by step, with no gaps?
3. Would I keep doing this if nobody were watching?
4. Do I measure myself against an outside standard?

If you can answer honestly, you are already moving toward the Seeker's road:

![A loop of four steps: follow your curiosity, practice weak spots, get real feedback, update and repeat](/assets/images/expertise/seekers-loop.svg)
*Interest picks the direction. Feedback from the real world checks the result.*

## 🌊 Closing Thought

Expertise is not a feeling of confidence, and it is not a performance for an audience. It is a library of patterns, built through practice and honest feedback, that lets you handle what is new and know where your map ends. Some people perform for others, some believe in themselves without testing it, and some keep checking against reality. The most reliable sign of that last group may be the willingness to say, "this is where my knowledge stops."
